How I think about product

Working principles

A few things I've picked up from years of being the person who finds where a product breaks — and started applying deliberately once I decided to build products, not just test them.

Product management isn't project management

Principle

Tracking tickets and hitting dates is coordination. The actual job is deciding what's worth building in the first place — and I want to own that decision, not just the delivery of it.

Start with the problem, not the fix

Principle

Before proposing a solution, I want to know: who is this for, what are they actually struggling with, and how will we know it worked. Every QA gap I ever found started with these three questions.

Talk to the people who use it

Principle

At ARIVE, the best defect reports came from imagining a real applicant's path through a form, not just the happy path in the spec. I don't trust a requirement I haven't pressure-tested against a real scenario.

Prototype before you commit

Principle

Cheap, early versions — a dashboard mock, a flow sketch, a rough model — surface the real questions faster than a polished spec. My academic projects were all built this way: rough first, refined once the idea proved out.

Every decision is a trade-off, so name it

Principle

Regulated products don't let you dodge trade-offs — compliance, usability, and timeline pull in different directions constantly. I'd rather state the trade-off explicitly than pretend a decision is free.

Measure it, don't just ship it

Principle

A feature request tells you what someone thinks they want. Usage data tells you what's actually happening. My crime-statistics dashboard project taught me this — the headline number and the real story were two different things.

MindSafe — Privacy-First Mental Health App

Shipped

UCD MSc assignment (IS40870) · Product concept, research & UX design

Designed a concept mental health app that predicts emotional triggers from real-life data — sleep, hydration, nutrition, menstrual cycle, work schedule — so users can understand patterns before a crisis rather than only after one. Started from a literature review of privacy failures in existing mental health apps (unclear policies, weak data rights, no offline mode) and used those findings to drive every product decision: opt-in-only data sharing with local-first storage, full user rights to view/export/delete their data, pseudonymous community features, and a controlled-access therapist collaboration flow. Delivered as a full case: problem framing, competitive positioning against existing apps, evidence-based design principles, and interactive mockups.

Impact: end-to-end product case study — from research and ethical design principles through to concrete interface decisions — grounded in 9 academic sources on mental-health-app privacy and usability.
product framing privacy by design competitive analysis UX mockups
